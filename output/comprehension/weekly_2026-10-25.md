# The week in AI — Sunday, October 25, 2026

*2026-10-19 to 2026-10-25 · claude-opus-5*

---

**The capacity race went demand-side and the market blinked — while the friction story moved from protests to contracts, courts, and compliance fines.**

### What happened

The week's real development sits at the intersection of two threads that are actually one: hyperscalers chasing capacity, and the grid pushing back. Google bought a gigawatt of distributed demand-response in PJM through Voltus — one of the largest VPPs in the US — in the same stretch of days that AEP signed 960MW of Indiana nuclear with Amazon and X-energy, and NextEra locked in $5.7B for federal energy hubs. The nuclear and VPP deals are nominally the same story (feed the load), but they are strategically opposite. Nuclear is a bet on new electrons arriving in the 2030s. A VPP is a bet that you can avoid needing them at the margin, right now, by paying distributed assets to get out of the way during peaks. That Google reached for the demand-side lever at gigawatt scale is the week's most legible signal that the supply-side answer is not arriving fast enough — a point LS Power's COO made explicitly when he said buildout cannot keep pace with demand. For anyone selling intelligence inside the power layer, this is the commercially relevant shift: flexibility and fast response are becoming procurable products, not engineering niceties.

The friction thread matured in parallel, and it matured away from the kind of friction that makes headlines toward the kind that makes contracts. Virginia's governor is using merger approval as a rate-freeze lever on Dominion-NextEra. Entergy and Google are fighting over the accounting of an Arkansas power deal. PJM's capacity price spikes are being re-read as a market-design defect rather than a market failure. A developer in Cardiff sized a gas plant at 49.9MW specifically to duck a Welsh approval threshold. And xAI took a $6.1M Mississippi fine for building without permits while Google was accused of clearing 250,000 trees in Finland. The through-line is that the fight over who pays for AI load has stopped being a referendum and become a series of specific, technical, jurisdictional negotiations — which is slower, less visible, and far more determinative of project timelines. The NYT finding that electricity-price anger is not yet swinging votes matters here too: it suggests the pressure routes through regulators, PUCs, and courts rather than through elections, which is the harder channel to predict and the one that binds.

Meanwhile the demand side of M4's own market got a reliability demonstration nobody asked for. A localized power event in Google Cloud's London region cascaded into outages at HSBC, Revolut, and Lloyds. That is a brand-new thread and it is worth sitting with: the failure propagated from a hardware/power fault at one facility into systemic disruption across a national banking sector. Every argument about faster interruption, arc-free switching, and sensing at the rack level is an argument about containment — keeping a local fault local. This incident is the cleanest available illustration of what happens when containment fails, and it will be cited by people selling resilience for a long while. Separately, OCP's Global Summit hit 15,000 attendees, up 38%, which is a momentum data point rather than a standards one, but it tells you the 800VDC conversation has an audience.

On the AI-at-large side, two threads converged into a single story about the economics of the current moment. Equity markets had their steepest AI-anxiety day in months, with coverage shifting from bubble punditry to 2008-comparison and tech-rotation framing; Meta's capex pushed cash flow negative, prompting a new share class; the neocloud debt anxiety spread past CoreWeave to the whole category. Set against that, the Anthropic numbers that circulated — $16B revenue against $9B compute cost, with the headline $80.7B loss being mostly a non-cash convertible-note charge — are the counterweight, and the more honest read is that model-layer unit economics look fine while the infrastructure-debt layer looks fragile. Those are different risks sitting in different balance sheets, and conflating them is how investors get the capex question wrong.

The cost-control thread deserves attention because it is where the model layer is quietly repricing. Anthropic support confirmed that the 5x/10x/20x plan multipliers apply to the five-hour session limit, not the weekly cap — meaning paying more raises your burst ceiling but not your actual throughput. That reframes the week's flood of complaints from entitled power users into something more structural: the vendor is rationing real capacity while selling the appearance of more of it. The LiveNerf project's day-29 verdict dovetails exactly — Opus 5.5's intelligence is unchanged, but usage caps appear to have been stealth-tightened. Read alongside Kimi K3.1 Pro arriving strong but throttled, the pattern is industry-wide: inference capacity, not model quality, is the binding constraint. Which is, of course, a power and data-center story wearing a pricing-page costume.

### What was noise

The reverse-engineering thread generated enormous volume and very little signal. Claude refusing the 'Reverse Engineer Anything' skill, an OpenAI account suspended for 'cyber abuse,' and TV-firmware hacks spreading to Ring doorbells all look like a narrative arc, but the week's own evidence undercuts the premise: a live bitcoin exploit bounty went unclaimed for hours, and HN readers never accepted the 'AI-driven' framing of the PHP RCE find in the first place. Cheap reverse-engineering of well-documented consumer firmware is real; the trivialization of novel exploitation is not demonstrated. Treat the guardrail-censorship story as a product-policy irritant, not an inflection in security economics.

Sweden 'banning' ChatGPT and Claude was the cleanest overhyped item of the week — both HN and Reddit landed on the same reading, that it is standard data-governance consolidation onto a contracted vendor, and Copilot runs on the same underlying models anyway. Similarly, Anthropic's 5% Public Benefit Fund pledge is pre-IPO optics and was read as such instantly; the pledge changes no governance mechanism. And the craftsmanship thread, now cycling through obituary essays, burnout posts, and disputed non-programmer mega-project claims, has stopped producing new information — the same positions are being restated with rising emotional volume. The one genuinely useful item buried in it was the admission that reviewers are waving through unreviewed AI commits for lack of token budget, which is a real operational risk rather than a philosophical complaint.

### What to watch

On capacity, the question is whether the Google-Voltus deal is a one-off or a template. Watch for a second hyperscaler procuring distributed or demand-response capacity at triple-digit-megawatt scale, and watch for how the flexibility is specified — response time, dispatch frequency, and whether any of it reaches inside the facility to rack-level load rather than stopping at the meter. That last distinction is where the power-electronics layer either becomes part of the product or stays outside it. On the friction side, the Entergy-Google accounting dispute and the Dominion-NextEra rate-freeze condition are the two to track: if either produces a published settlement with specific cost-allocation terms, that becomes the reference other utilities negotiate from, and it will shape what hyperscalers are willing to commit to contractually for years.

On the London outage, the thing that would make it matter is a root-cause disclosure naming the specific redundancy that failed — a UPS transfer, a switchgear fault, a breaker that did not clear fast enough. Without that, it stays an anecdote; with it, it becomes an argument. Watch also for whether any regulator (UK or EU financial supervisors, given which institutions went down) opens an inquiry into cloud-concentration resilience, because that would move power architecture from a procurement spec to a compliance one. On compute economics, the tell is whether Anthropic or a peer introduces a genuine mid-tier plan or publicly restructures how weekly caps work — that would confirm the rationing is capacity-driven rather than funnel-driven, and would be the clearest public admission yet that inference supply, not demand, is what is binding.
